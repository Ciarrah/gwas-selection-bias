Scenario

Figure 2(c). Genotype G affects disease incidence X and progression Y, and an unmeasured factor U affects both. Progression is studied only among cases (X = 1). Conditioning on X induces an association between incidence SNPs and U, and hence with Y: each SNP’s effect on progression is biased by about −b·βX, where βX is its effect on incidence.

SNPs affect incidence only (X_only), progression only (Y_only), both (both), or neither (null). The three scenarios differ in the SNPs affecting both:

  • c1 independent: effects on incidence and progression are uncorrelated;
  • c2 correlated: they are correlated (ρ = 0.6), and incidence-only SNPs dominate incidence heritability;
  • c3 sparse: few SNPs affect progression.

Correction methods for this design use summary statistics: an incidence GWAS in the full cohort and a progression GWAS among cases.

2. Diagnostic: negative control variants

Suppose 20 incidence-only SNPs are known from prior evidence not to affect progression. Collider bias predicts their progression effects to be opposite in sign to their incidence effects (when U raises both incidence and progression). A directional test across the variants is more powerful than counting p < 0.05, and λ is unstable with so few variants.

dir_test <- function(bx, z) {
  zd <- sum(sign(bx) * z) / sqrt(length(z))
  c(directional_z = zd, directional_p = 2 * pnorm(-abs(zd)))
}

3. Corrections

All three methods estimate the collider slope b and adjust every SNP: βY − b·βX. They differ in how b is estimated:

  • DHO: regression of βY on βX across all SNPs, with a Hedges-Olkin correction for regression dilution (indexevent package). Assumes the SNP effects on incidence and progression are uncorrelated.
  • Slope-Hunter: a mixture model that identifies the cluster of incidence-only SNPs and uses its slope (SlopeHunter package). Assumes incidence-only SNPs contribute at least as much to incidence heritability as pleiotropic SNPs.
  • MR-Horse adaptation: a Bayesian model with a horseshoe prior on direct effects on progression, allowing them to be sparse and correlated with incidence effects (JAGS).

The oracle is the Hedges-Olkin slope fitted only on the true incidence-only SNPs. It is a benchmark, not an exact truth, since it uses estimated effects from 50 to 100 SNPs.

fits <- setNames(lapply(scen, function(sc) {
  d <- dat[[sc]]
  s <- d$sumstats
  mhs <- mr_horse_slope(s$beta_x, s$se_x, s$beta_y, s$se_y,
                        use = s$p_x < 1e-3,
                        model_file = "5_correction-summary-level/mr_horse_model.jags")
  list(
    dho    = dho_correct(s$snp, s$beta_x, s$se_x, s$beta_y, s$se_y,
                         n = d$n_cases),
    sh     = slope_hunter_correct(s$snp, s$beta_x, s$se_x, s$beta_y, s$se_y,
                                  n = d$n_cases),
    mhs    = mhs,
    mh     = mr_horse_correct(s$snp, s$beta_x, s$se_x, s$beta_y, s$se_y,
                              mhs, n = d$n_cases),
    oracle = indexevent::indexevent(s$beta_x, s$se_x, s$beta_y, s$se_y,
                                    prune = which(s$group == "X_only"),
                                    method = "Hedges-Olkin")
  )
}), scen)

All three methods reduce the spurious associations of incidence-only SNPs towards the nominal 5%, but none is best in every scenario. DHO performs well when effects on incidence and progression are independent but under-corrects under correlated pleiotropy (c2). Slope-Hunter depends on incidence-only SNPs dominating incidence heritability. The MR-Horse adaptation is closest to the oracle under correlated pleiotropy, but its prior can over-correct when that correlation is absent (c1).
